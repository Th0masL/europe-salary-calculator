# Hungary employee payroll, income year 2026 — clean-room research dossier

**Research/access date:** 2026-10-04  
**Scope:** Hungarian-resident employee, age 30, single, no children, ordinary private-sector employment for the full year, regular gross cash salary only, and no personal, family, disability, young-person, mother, or other tax allowance. The employer is assumed to be in the ordinary social-contribution-tax regime, not KIVA, and to claim no employee-specific szocho credit. Amounts are Hungarian forint (HUF).

This is an independent reconstruction from Hungarian primary sources. It does not rely on calculator code, generated data, prior audits, or another country model.

## Executive result

For the stated facts, let annual gross cash employment income be `G`:

* personal income tax (SZJA): `15% × G`;
* employee social-security contribution (tb-járulék): `18.5% × G`;
* employee net: `G − 15%G − 18.5%G = 66.5% × G`;
* employer social contribution tax (szocho): `13% × G`;
* standard employer cost: `G + 13%G = 113% × G`.

There is no salary ceiling in those formulas. A “szocho upper limit” exists for specified natural-person capital/other income, but it does **not** cap the employer’s szocho on employment wages.

## 1. Employee deductions

### Personal income tax (SZJA)

Employment is a non-independent activity. In this cash-salary scenario the whole gross wage is income, and §8’s flat **15%** applies. There is no general zero band or progressive bracket and no upper cap.

The employee is 30, so the under-25 relief is inapplicable. The facts also expressly exclude the various family/mother/personal allowances. Therefore:

`SZJA = 0.15 × gross cash salary`.

### Employee social-security contribution

The unified employee contribution is **18.5%** of contribution-base income. NAV remits statutory shares of the unified amount among pension, health-insurance, and labour-market funds, but the employee-facing payroll charge is one 18.5% contribution; this dossier does not present the fund allocation as three additional deductions.

`Employee tb contribution = 0.185 × gross cash salary`.

The Tbj. contains no high-income contribution maximum for an ordinary employee. §42(1) even requires contribution in each insured relationship where more than one exists. The monthly lower floor is 30% of minimum wage, **HUF 96,840 in 2026**, with exceptions and day apportionment. It is irrelevant here because the lowest worked monthly salary is about HUF 666,667, well above both that floor and the full HUF 322,800 minimum wage.

### Net pay

No contribution is deducted from the PIT base in this scenario: PIT and tb contribution are parallel percentages of gross. Thus:

`Net = gross − 0.15×gross − 0.185×gross = 0.665×gross`.

## 2. Employer payroll burden

### Social contribution tax (szocho)

For ordinary employment, the szocho base equals the employee tb base and the rate is **13%**:

`Employer szocho = 0.13 × gross cash salary`.

There is no upper wage ceiling. The HUF 7,747,200 “adófizetési felső határ” in NAV’s booklet restricts szocho payable by a natural person on enumerated capital/other income. Wage income counts toward reaching that limit for those other incomes, but it does not stop the employer’s 13% wage szocho.

No szocho credit is modelled. Credits depend on facts not supplied—for example labour-market entrant status, occupation, changed work capacity, research employment, or qualifying training—and are employer-specific.

### Vocational-training contribution

There is **no separate 2026 payroll-rate charge**. NAV states that the standalone vocational-training contribution ceased on 2022-01-01. Qualifying vocational and dual training may instead reduce szocho, but no training facts are assumed.

### Rehabilitation contribution: employer-wide, not a salary percentage

This is not universally attributable to the employee. It applies when:

1. the employer’s average statistical headcount exceeds 25; and
2. qualifying employees with changed work capacity are below 5% of that headcount.

For 2026:

`annual rehabilitation contribution = missing qualifying headcount × HUF 2,905,200`.

The missing headcount can be fractional under the official statistical-headcount calculation. Because the employee count, eligible-worker count, exclusions, and employer identity are unknown, the worked employer costs **exclude** this levy. Adding HUF 2,905,200 to each employee would be wrong: it is charged per shortfall from an employer-wide quota, not per ordinary employee.

### Other employer-wide or contingent amounts

* KIVA is an alternative enterprise tax regime. The scenario deliberately assumes a standard szocho-paying employer; employer cost for a KIVA taxpayer cannot be inferred from employee salary alone.
* Tbj. §25(5) makes an employer contribute one third of qualifying sickness-benefit expense. No sickness occurs in the facts, so no amount is added.
* Corporate tax, local business tax, chamber contributions, occupational-health costs, and business insurance are not proportional statutory deductions from this employee’s wage and are not included as payroll charges.

## 3. Benefits, bonus and “13th salary”

The Labour Code’s general wage chapter requires a base wage and regulates performance pay and monthly payment; it does not establish a universal thirteenth-month wage for an ordinary private employee. Consequently, no extra month is invented.

If an employment contract or employer policy pays a cash thirteenth salary, premium, or bonus, Szja §25 expressly treats a bonus and other employment-connected receipts as employment income. It therefore enters gross when paid and attracts the same 15% SZJA, 18.5% employee contribution and 13% employer szocho, absent a specific exemption. With flat uncapped rates, whether the stated annual gross is paid over 12 or 13 instalments does not change the annual unrounded result.

Non-cash and fringe benefits cannot safely be treated as ordinary cash salary: the tax law has category-specific exemptions and bases. For example, NAV Booklet 49 notes a 1.18 multiplier for certain specified benefits. Benefits are excluded by the facts and require separate benefit-type inputs.

## 4. Calculation and reporting order

For each payroll payment:

1. determine taxable gross employment cash income;
2. subtract any applicable statutory tax-base allowance—zero here;
3. withhold SZJA at 15% of the PIT base;
4. withhold employee tb contribution at 18.5% of the contribution base;
5. net cash equals gross less those two withholdings;
6. employer calculates 13% szocho on the same base, before any employer-specific credit;
7. employer reports the month through the 2608 return.

The calculations below use exact annual multiplication. Actual payslips and the monthly return are in whole forints, so allocation of an annual salary into monthly whole-HUF payments and component rounding can move annual sums by a few forints. That is a payroll-timing artefact, not a different annual rate; no unsupported rounding convention is presented as universal.

* PIT: `40,000,000 × 0.15 = 6,000,000`;

## 6. Universal rules versus variable inputs

**Universal under the stated ordinary 2026 facts:** 15% SZJA, 18.5% employee tb, 13% employer szocho, no upper salary ceiling, monthly employer reporting, and no separate vocational-training contribution.

**Employer/person-specific and therefore excluded:** all employee tax allowances; pensioner status; szocho credits; KIVA status; rehabilitation quota liability; sickness-benefit contribution; fringe/non-cash benefit classification; and contractual bonus or thirteenth-month entitlement.

## 7. Evidence assessment and unresolved points

**Strongest evidence:** the consolidated Szja, Tbj and Szocho Acts directly establish the bases and 15%, 18.5%, and 13% rates. NAV’s dated 2026 szocho and rehabilitation booklets independently confirm the 2026 amounts and distinguish the employer-wide rehabilitation formula.

**Weakest evidence / deliberately unresolved:**

* The exact whole-forint rounding sequence used by every payroll product is not stated in the cited high-level statutory provisions. Worked figures therefore use exact annual rates and disclose potential monthly rounding differences.
* No headcount or changed-work-capacity workforce data were supplied, so rehabilitation contribution cannot be allocated. It is shown as a formula, not guessed.
* The employer’s KIVA status and eligibility for any szocho credit are unknown. The reproducible base scenario explicitly selects the ordinary szocho regime and zero credits.
* There is no universal private-sector “13th salary” amount to calculate. Any contractual cash amount is included in annual gross and taxed as employment income; benefit-specific exceptions require separate facts.
